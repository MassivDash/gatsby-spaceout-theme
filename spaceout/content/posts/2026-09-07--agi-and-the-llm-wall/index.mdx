# The Billion-Dollar Echo Chamber

## Introduction

If you sit in enough vendor meetings right now—or browse the timeline of any
heavily funded AI founder in 2026—you’ll hear the same underlying hum of
religious certainty: _Artificial General Intelligence is just a matter of
scale._

The pitch goes like this: we take an autoregressive language model, feed it the
entire internet, wire up a data centre the size of a small European country,
spend a billion dollars on training compute, and somewhere around the
hundred-trillion parameter mark, the model wakes up, stops hallucinating, and
starts doing our jobs.

I’ve been writing for a while about the gap between AI hype and engineering
reality. Most recently, I ran a benchmark proving that
[your shiny new model can't even add a column of numbers](/ai-cant-count-give-it-a-calculator)
without simulating it like a child doing long division out loud. But the AGI
conversation is different. It’s not just a debate over whether a model got the
maths wrong; it’s an architectural fallacy masquerading as a roadmap.

Right now, the industry is confusing the map for the territory. We are confusing
the ability to _describe_ reasoning with the ability to _actually reason_.

So let’s do what we always do here. Let’s strip away the marketing, look at the
hard data from 2025 and 2026, examine the actual mechanics of Large Language
Models, and ask the uncomfortable question: how far along the LLM route to AGI
are we actually, and is it even the right road?

## Defining the Goalpost

Before we talk about whether we can get there, we have to define "there."

The tech industry loves to define AGI as "passing the Turing test," or "scoring
in the 99th percentile on the bar exam," or "passing the medical boards." But as
I’ve learned from building
[local, sovereign agents in Rust](/local-ai-agents-llama-cpp-rust-ai-tools),
passing a static benchmark is a trick of memorisation and interpolation. If the
answers exist in the latent space of the training data, the model will find
them. That isn't intelligence; that's search compression.

Real AGI isn't a test-taker. It’s an entity that can:

1. **Operate in novel environments** where the rules aren't in its training
   data.
2. **Learn continuously** without needing a full, billion-dollar weight update.
3. **Reason non-linearly**, backtracking, simulating outcomes, and altering
   state before acting.
4. **Maintain agency** over long horizons without requiring a prompt to push it
   forward.

If you look at that list, you realise the problem: the standard LLM architecture
is fundamentally opposed to almost all of it.

## The "Data Wall" Reality Check

Let's start with the fuel that runs the entire operation: data.

Back in 2024, researchers at Epoch AI and other institutions started sounding
the alarm about the "data wall." The hypothesis was simple: we are going to run
out of high-quality, human-generated text on the internet to train these
increasingly massive models.

Fast forward to late 2026. The data wall isn't a hypothesis anymore; it’s the
defining constraint of the industry. The stock of high-quality public text is
effectively exhausted. You cannot scrape Reddit, Wikipedia, and Stack Overflow
again and expect a 10x capability leap.

To circumvent this, the industry pivoted to two strategies:

### 1. Synthetic Data (The Ouroboros)

Labs are now using frontier models to generate training data for the next
generation of models. On paper, it sounds elegant. In practice, it’s a
statistical trap. Training a model heavily on AI-generated data leads to "model
collapse" over multiple generations. The distribution tails shrink. The model
becomes an amplified version of its own biases. It learns how to write perfect
boilerplate code, but it loses the messy, human edge required to invent a
completely novel architectural pattern. Synthetic data is great for fine-tuning
specific tasks, but it does not expand the frontiers of general intelligence.

### 2. Inference-Time Scaling (Thinking Out Loud)

Because pre-training on raw data hit diminishing returns, the focus shifted to
"inference-time compute." Models like the OpenAI o-series (and now GPT-6) are
trained to spend more time "thinking" before they output a final answer. They
use iterative reasoning, chain-of-thought, and self-correction.

This is a massive improvement, and it's why these models are so much better at
coding and math than the models of 2024. But let's be clear about what is
actually happening: the model isn't gaining AGI. It is just being forced to
generate thousands of hidden tokens—simulating a reasoning loop—because its
underlying architecture still requires it to predict the next word to function.

## The ARC-AGI Illusion

To understand how deep the illusion goes, we need to look at François Chollet’s
ARC-AGI benchmark.

Unlike the Bar Exam or standard coding tests, ARC-AGI doesn't test memorisation.
It tests fluid intelligence. It presents the model with novel visual-logic
puzzles it has never seen before, requiring the model to deduce the underlying
rule from a few examples and apply it to a test case. It is designed to be easy
for humans and incredibly hard for AI.

In 2025, the top scores were hovering around the 24% mark. By September 2026,
the headlines exploded: OpenAI’s GPT-6 Astra achieved a **99.9% score** on
ARC-AGI-3.

AGI achieved, right?

Not exactly. If you dig into the paper, that 99.9% score was achieved using a
massive, highly complex "harness"—a specialized scaffolding of software wrapped
around the model that forced it to generate code, test the code, verify the
output against the puzzle, back-track, and try again, looping thousands of times
per puzzle.

When researchers stripped away the scaffolding and ran the same GPT-6 Astra
model under a standard, minimal-interface harness, the score plummeted to
**62.7%**.

The LLM itself wasn't reasoning. The _scaffolding_ was simulating reasoning by
wrapping the LLM in a brute-force search algorithm. The model was just
generating code variations. Hitting a benchmark through exhaustive programmatic
trial-and-error is not AGI. It is just better interpolation, wrapped in a
`while` loop.

## The Economics of the Plateau

This brings us to the financial reality, which is usually where the hype train
derails.

The exponential cost of training these models is colliding violently with the
logarithmic gains in capability. Let's look at the estimated training costs for
flagship models over the last few years:

| Year    | Era           | Estimated Training Cost | Notable Capability Leap                        |
| :------ | :------------ | :---------------------- | :--------------------------------------------- |
| 2020    | GPT-3         | ~$4 Million             | Few-shot prompting, basic code generation      |
| 2023    | GPT-4         | ~$100 Million           | Advanced reasoning, passing standardized tests |
| 2025    | GPT-5 / o1    | ~$300 Million           | Inference-time reasoning, advanced math/logic  |
| 2026/27 | GPT-6 / Astra | >$1 Billion             | Saturated static benchmarks, agentic workflows |

Training costs are climbing by roughly 2.5x to 3.5x per year. We are approaching
the billion-dollar threshold for a single training run. But are we getting a
billion dollars worth of new intelligence?

The industry's own actions give us the answer. Notice how every major provider
now ships "tiered" model families? We have the Frontier tier (GPT-6, Claude 4.5
Opus), the Mid tier, and the Budget tier (Nano/Mini).

Providers do this because the flagship models have become astronomically
expensive to run, and for 95% of real-world enterprise tasks—routing data,
summarising text, basic code autocomplete—the budget models from 2025 are
already perfectly adequate. You don't need a billion-dollar supercomputer to
parse a JSON payload.

The performance at the very top of the leaderboard has converged. The massive
capability leaps we saw from 2020 to 2023 have slowed into an expensive,
incremental grind. The industry is spending exponential capital for fractional
gains on edge-case reasoning, hoping that if they just push hard enough, the
model will suddenly learn to think.

## Why Next-Token Prediction Can't Reason (The Architecture)

Let me be precise about what an LLM actually is, because the anthropomorphism is
killing our ability to engineer properly.

An LLM is a phenomenal, high-dimensional statistical map of human language. It
predicts the most likely next token based on the context window. It doesn't
"think." It interpolates. When it gives you a brilliant architectural design,
it’s because a brilliant architectural design exists in the latent space it
mapped from human data.

But true AGI requires breaking free from the context window entirely. Here is
why the architecture fails:

1. **The compute asymmetry:** As I showed in the
   [counting experiment](/ai-cant-count-give-it-a-calculator), LLMs have no
   internal state memory that persists outside of the context window. They
   cannot update their internal model of the world without emitting a token. If
   an AGI needs to silently simulate a complex 10-year business strategy, an LLM
   has to generate tokens for every single micro-step of that simulation. It is
   computationally absurd.
2. **The novelty cliff:** LLMs interpolate beautifully between known concepts.
   They extrapolate terribly. Put an LLM in a genuinely novel situation with no
   prior training data—like a newly invented programming language with zero
   internet footprint—and it hallucinates confidently. AGI requires robust
   extrapolation.
3. **The static brain problem:** When an LLM learns something new in its context
   window (e.g., you paste in documentation), that knowledge vanishes the moment
   the session ends. You can bolt on RAG (Retrieval-Augmented Generation) and
   vector databases—which I advocate for highly in local setups—but that is
   external tooling, not internal learning. The core weights remain frozen in
   time, unchanged until the next hundred-million-dollar training run.

## What Actually Gets Us There?

If LLMs are just the language centre of the brain, what does the rest of the
architecture look like? We aren't going to reach AGI by making the language
centre bigger. We have to build the rest of the system.

Here is the architectural shift I'm watching for over the next five years:

1. **Neuro-symbolic systems:** We need to combine the fuzzy, probabilistic
   brilliance of neural networks with the hard, deterministic logic of symbolic
   systems. Give the AI a calculator; don't ask it to be one. Let the LLM handle
   the semantic parsing, and hand the logic over to a deterministic solver.
2. **Continuous local learning:** We need architectures where weights update
   dynamically in real-time based on successful outcomes. An agent needs to
   learn that a specific bash command fails on a specific server, and remember
   it tomorrow, without needing a billion-dollar cluster to re-train its
   weights.
3. **True world models:** We must move beyond text prediction to predicting
   physics, state changes, and causal relationships. Video models are a baby
   step toward this, but they are still largely statistical. An AGI needs a
   grounded understanding that if it deletes a database table, the application
   crashes. Right now, it just knows that the word "delete" is often followed by
   the word "error" in its training set.

## Final Thoughts

Are we close to AGI? No.

Are we close to highly capable, hyper-competent narrow agents? Yes. We are
already there. If you build them right—with strict boundaries, typed toolchains,
and local control, exactly like we explored in the
[Local AI Agents piece](/local-ai-agents-llama-cpp-rust-ai-tools)—they will
absolutely change how software is built and businesses are run.

But the idea that an LLM will magically transition into Artificial General
Intelligence just by reading more synthetic data and running on a larger GPU
cluster is an illusion. We haven't built a mechanical brain; we've built a
mechanical parrot with an eidetic memory and a silver tongue.

The next leap in intelligence isn't going to come from a bigger data centre.
It’s going to come from an entirely new architecture that abandons next-token
prediction as the core engine of thought. Until then, stop waiting for the API
to wake up, and start building the guardrails.
